West Little Rock Property Value History: What $350,000 Bought in Foxcroft vs. Today

In 2010, $350,000 in the Foxcroft neighborhood of West Little Rock bought a one-of-a-kind, architect-built contemporary. Sixteen years later that same figure buys something much more ordinary — and the gap between those two facts says a lot about how this market actually works.

The 2010 benchmark

In 2010, The New York Times ran one of its recurring "What You Get for…" real estate columns at the $350,000 price point. One of the homes featured was in Foxcroft, a cul-de-sac neighborhood in West Little Rock. It was a custom contemporary built in 1973 by a local architect, and the listing details that made the column were the kind you don't find in a subdivision: a revolving entry door salvaged from an old Little Rock courthouse, spiral staircases, decks on multiple levels, a gazebo, and a rooftop terrace.

That house is useful as a market benchmark for two reasons. It puts a hard dollar figure and a hard date on a specific West Little Rock property, and it is exactly the sort of home that doesn't behave like the rest of the market — which turns out to be the more interesting half of the story.

What happened to Little Rock values after 2010

The cleanest long-run measure for this market is the Federal Housing Finance Agency's all-transactions house price index for the Little Rock–North Little Rock–Conway metro. It tracks repeat sales of the same properties, so it isn't distorted by changes in the mix of what happens to be selling in a given year.

Run the 2010 listing price through that index and the arithmetic is straightforward. A property worth $350,000 in 2010 that simply tracked the metro average would be worth roughly $573,000 today. Pointed the other direction: $350,000 in today's money is the equivalent of about $214,000 in 2010 — which, in 2010 Little Rock, was a comfortable but unremarkable house.

Compared with the coastal markets that doubled and then doubled again over the same stretch, 3.3% a year sounds modest. For Central Arkansas it is the thing that makes the market work: steady, unspectacular appreciation without the whipsaw. Owners here generally haven't been handed life-changing equity by the calendar alone, and they also haven't been wiped out.

Why Foxcroft and West Little Rock didn't move in lockstep

A metro index is an average, and averages hide the interesting cases. Three things pulled West Little Rock away from the metro number after 2010.

New construction moved further west

Chenal and the development west of it absorbed most of the new high-end construction in Pulaski County. Buyers with $500,000 and up got a genuine choice between a 1970s custom home on an established lot and new construction with current layouts and current mechanicals. That choice capped what the older inventory could ask.

Established lots held their own

Working against that, neighborhoods like Foxcroft kept something new construction can't manufacture: mature trees, larger and irregular lots, cul-de-sac streets, and the particular quiet that comes with a neighborhood that finished building out decades ago. That premium is real and it is why the older West Little Rock pockets didn't simply get left behind.

Mechanical age became the dividing line

By 2026, a 1973 house is on its third roof and likely its second or third HVAC system. Where that work was done on schedule, the homes track the market. Where it was deferred, the gap between a maintained house and a tired one on the same street can run well into six figures — far more than the architecture accounts for either way.

The problem with pricing a genuinely unusual house

Here is where a home like the Foxcroft contemporary stops following the index altogether. Distinctive architecture does not reliably translate into a distinctive price, and the reasons are mechanical rather than aesthetic.

  • The buyer pool is small. Spiral staircases, multi-level decks and a rooftop terrace are a genuine draw for a narrow set of buyers and a genuine objection for everyone else — families with small children, buyers planning to age in place, anyone who looks at a rooftop terrace and sees a maintenance bill.
  • Appraisers have nothing to work from. An appraisal needs comparable sales. When a house is the only one of its kind for miles, the appraiser reaches for conventional homes nearby and the custom work is valued at close to nothing. Deals die at the appraisal far more often than at the showing.
  • Lenders get cautious. Salvaged architectural elements, non-standard stairs and unconventional roof structures all invite scrutiny on an inspection, and conventional financing is where that scrutiny lands hardest.
  • Days on market compound. A distinctive home that sits gets re-listed, price-cut and stale, and the eventual sale price reflects the staleness rather than the house.

None of this means an architecturally significant home can't sell well on the open market. With the right agent, a realistic price and the patience to wait for the one buyer who wants exactly that house, listing is usually the way to the highest number.

What it means is that the traditional path asks for time and tolerance for uncertainty. Sellers working against a deadline — a probate with heirs in three states, an estate where the carrying costs are eating the proceeds, a relocation that already has a start date — often find that the open market's timeline and their own don't match. That is the situation where working with a direct buyer is worth pricing out alongside a listing, because it trades some of the top-end price for a closing date you can actually plan around.

What $350,000 buys in West Little Rock in 2026

With the metro median listing price at roughly $295,000, $350,000 today sits above the midpoint but well short of the top of the market. In practical terms that is a well-kept four-bedroom in an established West Little Rock neighborhood, or a smaller newer home further west, or — still — something genuinely unusual that has been sitting long enough for the price to come down to meet the buyer pool.

That last category is the one worth watching. The homes that made a 2010 magazine column are now 50-plus years old, and many are changing hands through estates rather than through move-up sellers. The condition spread inside that group is enormous, and so is the spread in what they sell for.

Common questions

How much have Little Rock home values gone up since 2010?

About 64% on the FHFA metro index between 2010 and 2025, which works out to roughly 3.3% a year compounded. Individual neighborhoods and individual houses vary widely around that figure.

Is an architect-designed home harder to sell here?

Usually, yes — not because buyers don't want it, but because the buyer pool is smaller, the appraisal is harder to support, and lenders ask more questions. The right buyer exists; finding them takes longer.

Does a cash buyer pay market value?

No, and we'd rather say so plainly. A cash offer comes in below full retail value. The discount is the trade for no repairs, no commissions, no showings and a closing date that holds. If you have time and the house is in good shape, listing it will almost always net you more.
